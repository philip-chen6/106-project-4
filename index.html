What does a hit song look like when skipping is one tap away?

We split 24,206 Billboard Hot 100 songs into three eras: pre-streaming (before 2010), streaming growth (2010–2019), and streaming native (2020 onward). As you scroll, the chart updates — or click any feature above to explore on your own.

Hits got shorter

Average song length fell from 3.66 minutes before streaming to 3.20 minutes in the native era — a 12% drop. When listeners can skip instantly, the hook arrives faster.

They became more danceable

Danceability climbed from 0.59 pre-streaming to 0.67 in the native era. Playlist culture and shuffle listening reward rhythm-forward tracks that keep thumbs from hitting skip.

Energy rose with streaming

Energy peaked during streaming growth at 0.67, well above the pre-streaming average of 0.61. Hits became more immediate and intense — built to grab attention in the first few seconds.

They got louder too

Loudness jumped from −9.3 dB pre-streaming to −6.0 dB during growth. The loudness war didn't end — chart-toppers simply punch harder through phone speakers and earbuds.

Acousticness collapsed

While duration and danceability moved together, acousticness broke away — dropping from 0.32 to 0.19 during streaming growth. Fully produced, electronic-leaning tracks replaced unplugged chart moments.

Valence fell even as danceability rose

Here's the tension: hits grew more danceable but less happy. Valence dropped from 0.63 pre-streaming to 0.46 in the native era. The modern hit hooks you with rhythm, not sunshine.
